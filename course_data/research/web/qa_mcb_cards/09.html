The 27-hole Paa-Ko Ridge Golf Club is set under the watchful guard of the Sandia Mountains. With three loops of nine holes, there are numerous playing configurations especially when you factor in five different sets of tees on each hole. Choose your tee carefully as this monster stretches out beyond 7,500 yards from the tips.

Holes are routed around arroyos, rocky outcrops and a variety of native vegetation with fairways laid out in quite wooded surroundings at elevations between 6,500 and 7,000 feet above sea level. The original 18-hole course opened in 2000 with the additional nine added five years later.

One of the most difficult holes on the property is the par three 4th – appropriately named “Dye-abolical,” – where the tee shot plays across an arroyo to what's reckoned to be the largest putting surface in the state of New Mexico. As this triple-tiered green measures 100 yards from back to front with a drop of 13 feet in between, pin placement is all important in the quest to secure a score of three or better at this hole.

Designed by the Finger Dye Spann firm of architects, Paa-Ko – meaning the root of the cottonwood tree in ancient Pueblo – is undoubtedly New Mexico’s finest course and it’s hard to know which two of the three loops of nine make the better course, so we suggest you play all 27 dramatic holes. We doubt you’ll get better value for money within a 500-mile radius.

10 of the Most Expensive Golf Courses in the World You Can Actually Play

Golf has never been an inexpensive sport, but some courses command green fees that take things to another level. From famous championship venues and bucket-list links courses to luxurious resort layouts, playing a round at the top end of the market can cost over £1,000. But which courses are the most expensive, and what do golfers actually get for their money? We've looked at visitor green fees around the world to find some of the most expensive rounds available to golfers who can book independently, without needing a membership, invitation or compulsory resort stay. From Scotland and Spain to the United States, these are ten courses where a round comes with a particularly hefty price tag. Trump Turnberry – Ailsa Course (Scotland) World ranking: 12th | Scotland ranking: 3rd | Green fees: £1000 The Ailsa Course is one of the world's most recognisable links layouts, occupying a spectacular stretch of Ayrshire coastline with views across the Firth of Clyde towards Ailsa Craig. Its iconic lighthouse, which sits between the ninth and tenth holes, provides one of the most photographed backdrops in golf. A four-time Open Championship venue, it is perhaps best remembered for the 1977 'Duel in the Sun' between Tom Watson and Jack Nicklaus. A major renovation by Martin Ebert in 2016 introduced five new holes along the coastline. At £1,000 for a peak morning tee time, the Ailsa is among the most expensive rounds available to visiting golfers. The rate is for non-residents, with afternoon prices considerably lower. The green fee covers 18 holes and access to the practice facilities, while caddies, club hire, trolleys and buggies are charged separately. Caddies start at £85, with a recommended gratuity of £30. Wynn Golf Club (Nevada, USA) Nevada ranking: 7th | Green fees: US$1000 (£745) Set behind the Wynn Las Vegas resort on the famous Strip, Wynn Golf Club offers a very different setting from the desert courses typically associated with the city. Originally designed by Tom Fazio in collaboration with Steve Wynn, the course reopened in 2019 following a major redesign by Fazio and his son Logan. Its lush landscaping, streams and waterfalls create a striking contrast with the surrounding hotels and casinos, while more than half the holes now offer views of the nearby Sphere. The peak green fee reaches US$1,000 during the holiday season and Las Vegas Grand Prix, although rates fluctuate and are lower at other times of year. The fee includes 18 holes, a shared golf cart, a mandatory caddie, soft drinks and food served during the round. Complimentary Callaway rental clubs are also available. Alcohol and caddie gratuities are extra. Real Club Valderrama (Andalucía, Spain) World ranking: 100th | Spain ranking: 1st | Green fees: €700 (£584) Located in Sotogrande on Spain’s Costa del Sol, Valderrama is one of European golf’s most recognisable venues. Designed by Robert Trent Jones Sr. and refined after Jaime Ortiz-Patiño acquired the property in 1985, it became the first course in continental Europe to host the Ryder Cup, staging the 1997 contest under the captaincy of Seve Ballesteros. The course has also hosted numerous Volvo Masters and DP World Tour events. The 2027 visitor green fee is €700 for 18 holes. A forecaddie is mandatory for every group, costing an additional €70 plus gratuity. Buggy hire is €70, electric trolleys €25 and club rental €50. Hand trolleys and practice balls are complimentary. Although Valderrama is a private members’ club, visitors can book independently, with tee times currently restricted to Monday–Thursday between noon and 1pm. TPC Sawgrass – Stadium Course (Florida, USA) USA ranking: 66th | Green fees: Starting at US$775 (£577) The Stadium Course at TPC Sawgrass is one of the most recognisable layouts in American golf, best known as the annual host of THE PLAYERS Championship. Designed by Pete and Alice Dye, it opened in 1980 and is famous for its demanding island-green 17th hole. The course was built to challenge the world’s best, but is also available to visiting golfers. The peak-season green fee starts at US$775 per player from September to May, with summer rates starting at $550. TPC Sawgrass uses dynamic pricing, so actual tee times can cost more than the advertised starting rate. The fee includes 18 holes, a shared golf cart, a forecaddie for the group and a warm-up bag of practice balls. The suggested forecaddie gratuity of $40 per player is extra. Golfers wishing to walk must upgrade to a walking caddie, starting at $85 per player for a shared caddie. Trump International Golf Links – Old Course (Aberdeen, Scotland) World ranking: 77th | Scotland ranking: 11th | Green fees: £560 Set among the towering dunes of the Menie Estate, just north of Aberdeen, Trump International’s Old Course is a modern links designed by Martin Hawtree and opened in 2012. The layout stretches along three miles of North Sea coastline, with dunes reaching up to 100 feet and several holes offering dramatic views across the surrounding landscape. It hosted the DP World Tour’s Scottish Championship in 2025, putting it on the professional golf calendar for the first time. The 2027 peak-season green fee is £560 for 18 holes. The course is open to non-resident visitors, who can book directly without a hotel stay or member introduction. Caddies are optional, costing £80 per player plus gratuity for a traditional caddie, or £30 per player plus gratuity for a forecaddie. Club hire starts at £70, push trolleys at £10 and electric trolleys at £35. Buggy hire is £75, subject to course regulations. Kingsbarns Golf Links (Fife, Scotland) World ranking: 43rd | Scotland ranking: 7th | Green fees: £524+ Kingsbarns sits on the Fife coastline, around seven miles south of St Andrews, with almost every hole offering views across the North Sea. Opened in 2000, the course was designed by Kyle Phillips and has quickly established itself as one of Scotland’s leading modern links. It has also become a regular co-host of the Alfred Dunhill Links Championship, alongside the Old Course at St Andrews and Carnoustie. The 2027 visitor green fee is £524, rising to £590 for the exceptional period of 12–20 July, when The Open returns to St Andrews. The fee includes 18 holes and use of the full practice and clubhouse facilities. Caddies are available for an additional £80, plus any gratuity, while buggies are restricted to golfers with a medical need. Pebble Beach Golf Links (California, USA) World ranking: 27th | USA ranking: 14th | Green fees: US$695+ (£518) Pebble Beach is one of the most famous courses in world golf, occupying a dramatic stretch of Pacific coastline on California’s Monterey Peninsula. Opened in 1919, the course was designed by Jack Neville and Douglas Grant, with later changes by H. Chandler Egan helping shape the layout played today. Its most spectacular stretch runs from the seventh to the 10th, where holes hug the coastline, while the 18th provides one of golf’s most recognisable finishing holes. Pebble Beach has hosted six U.S. Opens and is a regular venue for the PGA Tour’s AT&T Pebble Beach Pro-Am. The 2026/27 green fee is US$695. Non-resort guests must also pay a $60 cart fee, bringing the total to $755. Caddies are optional, costing $175 for a single bag or $250 for a double bag, while club hire is $120 per bag plus tax. Pebble Beach is open to the public, but securing a tee time independently is not straightforward: non-resort guests can only book 24 hours before play, subject to availability. Resort guests can book further ahead, but a minimum two-night stay is generally required. Trump National Doral Golf Club – Blue Monster (Florida, USA) Florida ranking: 35th | Green fees: US$695 (£518) The Blue Monster is the signature course at Trump National Doral, a resort just west of Miami. Designed by Dick Wilson and opened in 1962, it became a regular stop on the PGA Tour, hosting the Doral Open and later the WGC-Cadillac Championship. Gil Hanse led a major redesign in 2014, lengthening the course and reshaping its greens, bunkers and water hazards. Its closing stretch is particularly demanding, with water coming into play on several holes. The 2026 peak-season green fee is $695, with a golf cart included. A forecaddie is mandatory, costing an additional $37 per group, with a recommended gratuity of $40 per player. Taxes are also extra. Summer rates drop to $395, while twilight rates are lower still. Gleneagles – King's, Queen’s and PGA Centenary (Perth & Kinross, Scotland) Green fees: £500 Gleneagles is home to three championship courses, each offering a different test of golf in the Perthshire countryside. The King's and Queen's were designed by James Braid and C.K. Hutchison, opening in 1919 and 1917 respectively, while the PGA Centenary was designed by Jack Nicklaus and opened in 1993. The latter hosted the 2014 Ryder Cup and 2019 Solheim Cup, while the King's has staged the Scottish Open and Senior Open. The 2027 peak-season visitor green fee is £500 per round on all three courses, with July the most expensive month. The fee covers 18 holes, while caddies cost £80 per player or £122 for a group forecaddie for four. Club hire starts at £55, pull trolleys cost £10 and electric trolleys £28. Buggies are available on the PGA Centenary for £60, but are otherwise restricted to medical exemptions. The Greenbrier – The Old White (West Virginia, USA) West Virginia ranking: 3rd | Green fees: US$665 (£495) The Old White was the first of three courses built at The Greenbrier resort, opening in 1913. Designed by Charles Blair Macdonald, with later modifications by his associate Seth Raynor, it is a classic parkland layout with tight, tree-lined fairways and small, well-protected greens. Several holes take inspiration from famous designs in Britain and Ireland, including the Redan at North Berwick, the Alps at Prestwick and the Eden at St Andrews. It has hosted both the PGA Tour and LIV Golf. The highest published non-resident green fee is US$665 for a morning tee time on Friday or Saturday between May 1 and October 25. The fee covers 18 holes, but taxes and the Historic Preservation Fund (a 9.75% fee included to safeguard and enhance the landscapes, buildings, and amenities that have shaped The Greenbrier) are additional. A forecaddie costs $45 per person, with an $85 minimum per group, while a walking caddie is $100 per bag. Club hire is $75. Does an Expensive Green Fee Mean a Better Golf Course?

As green fees at some of Britain and Ireland’s most celebrated courses continue to climb, Top100 Golf Courses' lead panellist Jasper Miners argues that golfers need to separate the quality of the course from the price of getting onto it. A round of golf at one of Great Britain and Ireland’s most prestigious courses can now cost £400, £500 or even £1,000. But does paying more guarantee a better golfing experience? For Top100 Golf Courses' lead panellist Jasper Miners, the answer is no. While some of the world’s finest courses command premium green fees for good reason, he believes golfers can too easily confuse price with quality. “Price can be exclusivity, it can be quality, it can be difficulty to access, it can also just be damn good branding,” he says. “I've played a lot of golf courses that are very expensive to play and I'd never want to play them again.” The distinction is becoming increasingly relevant as visitor fees at the top end of the game continue to rise. Research published by National Club Golfer in 2026 found that 15 courses in its Great Britain and Ireland Top 100 list charged £400 or more for a peak-summer round, up from eight the previous year. Turnberry's Ailsa Course retained its £1,000 headline fee, while Royal Birkdale's rose to £495. Those prices reflect more than the quality of the golf itself. Reputation, international demand, limited visitor access and the appeal of playing a famous course all contribute to what golfers are willing to pay. “People spend money on an experience, and it's cognitive dissonance: you convince yourself that it's good,” he says. Scotland shows the gap between price and quality Scotland offers a useful illustration of the difference between headline prices and the wider market. Top100's analysis of 94 ranked Scottish courses with published summer visitor fees in 2026 found a median price of £140, compared with an average of £194. The difference is significant: a relatively small number of expensive courses pull the average upwards, while more than half of the courses in the analysis could be played for £140 or less. Twenty-seven charged under £100. That doesn't mean £140 is inexpensive for every golfer, nor does it suggest that all courses offer the same experience. It does, however, challenge the idea that playing highly ranked golf necessarily requires a four-figure budget. The price of some famous venues has risen considerably. The Old Course at St Andrews, for example, went from a summer green fee of £195 in 2021 to £355 in 2026. Yet Scotland's wider selection of highly ranked courses offers alternatives at a fraction of that price. The same principle applies beyond Scotland. Top100's list of the ten cheapest visitor-accessible courses in its current World Top 100 includes Australia's St Andrews Beach at £74 and Bethpage Black in New York, a major championship venue, from £106. These examples do not mean that every lower-priced course offers the same facilities or experience as the most expensive venues. They do show that a high ranking and a substantial green fee do not always go hand in hand. The market, however, does not operate on quality alone. Miners points to Scotscraig Golfing Club as an example of how perceptions can change alongside pricing. He says the course has gone from attracting relatively little visitor attention to charging £225 and selling out. “In America, if you're good, you can charge more, so price becomes equated with quality,” he says. “Scotland has been a beneficiary of that.” He believes the relationship between price and demand can become self-reinforcing. A higher fee can contribute to a course's image as an exclusive destination, and that exclusivity can itself become part of the attraction. The challenge for lesser-known courses For courses without the international recognition of St Andrews, Muirfield or Royal County Down, competing for attention can be more difficult. A lower green fee may make a course attractive to golfers looking for value, but it can also leave it competing against venues whose reputations have been built over generations. Miners believes independent rankings and editorial coverage have a role to play in identifying courses that deserve attention, regardless of what they charge. “Hopefully, a website like Top100 is doing that work where we're going and saying, actually, this is not expensive and that's not part of our criteria, this is worth going to play,” he says. Simply increasing prices to appear more prestigious is not a universal solution, either. A strategy that works in a high-demand Scottish golf destination may not work in Wales, where raising green fees substantially will not necessarily persuade more people to visit. For clubs, the challenge is to establish what their course offers, understand their market and communicate its appeal. A famous name can command a premium, but a lesser-known venue may have more to gain by demonstrating the quality of its golf and the value of the overall experience. Golf is a business, too There is another side to the debate. While golfers may understandably want affordable access to good courses, clubs have to cover the costs of maintaining their facilities and operating their businesses. “I don't think [the industry needs to do more to challenge the idea that the best experiences are the most expensive],” Miners says. “As much as we don't want to admit it, golf is a capitalist sport.” He points out that many members' clubs depend on visitor income to support their finances, particularly when membership fees and subscriptions alone are insufficient to cover operating costs. “There are very few clubs that can run on a very modest budget with a very low membership that doesn't need outside play,” he says. That commercial reality makes it difficult to argue that clubs should simply charge less. Golfers in Britain and Ireland may be accustomed to playing regularly at prices that seem modest by international standards, but maintaining a golf course is expensive, and visitor income can be essential to a club's survival. Where demand is strong and tee times are limited, higher visitor fees may be a rational business decision. “If their tee sheet is full and they can charge a lot of money for people to come play, good for them,” he says. “The issue is mine, not theirs.” The question, then, is not whether every expensive course should lower its fees, or whether lower-priced courses should charge more. It is whether golfers can look beyond the number on the booking page and judge a course on its own merits. For golfers planning their next trip, that means looking beyond the obvious names. For clubs, it means recognising that price is only one part of the proposition. And for the industry, it is a reminder that the most expensive round and the best golfing experience are not necessarily the same thing.

10 of the Best Places to Play Night Golf Around the World

Golf doesn't always have to finish when the sun goes down. From floodlit championship courses in the Middle East to late-night rounds beneath the midnight sun in Norway, there are plenty of ways to play after dark – whether you're squeezing in an extra round on holiday or looking for a different kind of golf experience. We've picked out 10 venues around the world offering something beyond a conventional daytime round, from full-length courses under floodlights to short-course challenges and one-off events that put a new spin on night golf. Carya Golf Club (Antalya, Turkey) Carya became the first golf course in Europe to install floodlights across all 18 holes, giving golfers the chance to play a full championship round after dark. Designed by Peter Thomson and his team, the par-72 layout brings a taste of traditional English heathland golf to Belek, with sandy terrain, heather and fairways winding through pine and eucalyptus trees. The floodlights are positioned high enough for golfers to “follow the flight of the ball just as you would if playing in daylight”, according to the club. Evening tee times also offer an alternative to daytime play during the summer heat, making Carya a useful addition to a golf holiday on Turkey's Mediterranean coast. Emirates Golf Club – Faldo Course (Dubai, United Arab Emirates) The Faldo Course at Emirates Golf Club is one of Dubai's established night-golf venues, with LED floodlights covering all 18 holes. Originally opened as The Wadi in 1996, the par-72 course was redesigned by Nick Faldo in 2005 and renamed in his honour a year later. Measuring more than 7,000 yards from the back tees, it offers a full-length championship round after dark, with water hazards and strategically placed bunkers providing plenty of challenges along the way. The floodlighting allows golfers to tackle the entire layout after sunset, rather than being limited to a shorter course or a handful of illuminated holes. For visitors to Dubai, it's an opportunity to play a championship course at a different time of day. Contact the club to confirm current night-golf tee times and availability. Grass Clippings at Rolling Hills (Arizona, USA) Grass Clippings at Rolling Hills has turned night golf into a central part of its identity. The 18-hole course plays as an executive layout during the day, but after dark it switches to a par-54 format made up entirely of par threes. The course is illuminated seven days a week, from dusk until 1am, with nine-hole tee times available after 10pm. The venue is also home to The Grass League, a short-course golf league whose events are broadcast live on Golf Channel twice a year. With views across the Phoenix skyline and a lively atmosphere built around golf under the lights, Grass Clippings offers more than a conventional evening round. Long Bien Golf Course (Hanoi, Vietnam) Designed by Nelson & Haworth, Long Bien Golf Course has 27 holes across three nine-hole loops on gently undulating terrain, with bunkers and water hazards adding to the challenge. Located within Hanoi, it is around 20–30 minutes by car from the Old Quarter, making it a convenient option for visitors staying in the city. The course has a dedicated lighting system for night golf, allowing players to tee off later and finish their rounds under the lights. With three nine-hole loops, there are different combinations to play, although golfers should check with the club to confirm which nines are illuminated on their chosen evening. Ba Na Hills Golf Club (Đà Nẵng, Vietnam) Set among forested hills beneath the Ba Na mountain range, Ba Na Hills Golf Club offers a striking setting for a round after dark. Designed by Luke Donald in collaboration with IMG, the 18-hole, par-72 course features undulating greens and a backdrop of native forest, around 30 minutes from central Da Nang. Floodlights cover all 18 holes, with the course lit until 9pm on selected days. The club describes the ideal experience as starting the front nine as dusk settles over the mountains before finishing the back nine under the lights. Night golf can also be arranged for groups of 40 or more. Rachakram Golf Club & Resort (Phra Nakhon Si Ayutthaya, Thailand) Around 45 minutes from Bangkok, Rachakram Golf Club & Resort offers an 18-hole, par-72 course where golfers can play well into the night. Designed by Dr Sukitti Klangvisai, the layout has a links-style feel, with relatively open fairways, undulating terrain and fast greens. Water is a recurring challenge, including blind carries and several demanding par threes, while all of the par fives measure more than 500 yards. Night golf is available daily, with the last tee-off listed at around 7pm and play continuing until midnight. At 1,200 baht (approximately £27), Rachakram offers an affordable way to play a full 18-hole round under the lights. The on-site resort also means golfers can stay overnight rather than make the journey back to Bangkok after a late finish. Kelab Golf Sultan Abdul Aziz Shah (Selangor, Malaysia) Kelab Golf Sultan Abdul Aziz Shah (KGSAAS) is a 27-hole facility in Shah Alam, just outside Kuala Lumpur, with three nine-hole loops designed by Peter Thomson and Michael Wolveridge in the mid-1990s. The Sultan, Alam Shah and President nines offer different combinations for an 18-hole round, with the par-five second on the Sultan loop regarded as the course’s signature hole. Known as the “Home of Night Golfing”, KGSAAS was the first course in Malaysia to introduce floodlit play. Its 27-hole layout gives golfers the flexibility to play after dark, making it an option for visitors looking to fit a round into an evening in Kuala Lumpur. Cloud 9 at Angel Park Golf Club (Nevada, USA) Cloud 9 at Angel Park is a 12-hole par-3 course inspired by famous holes from around the world. Designed by Bob Cupp and John Fought, the layout takes inspiration from celebrated holes including "Island Green" at TPC Sawgrass, the "Postage Stamp" at Royal Troon and the "Valley of Sin" at St Andrews. Nine of the 12 holes are illuminated for evening play, allowing golfers to tackle the short course under the lights. It's a different proposition from playing a full-length championship course at night, with the shorter holes offering a chance to work on the short game in a more relaxed format. Cloud 9 is open for daytime play too, and tee times for evening rounds can be booked through Angel Park. Trump International Golf Links - Old Course (Aberdeen, Scotland) Trump International's Old Course is a championship links designed by Martin Hawtree, stretching through towering sand dunes along the North Sea coast, around 10 miles north of Aberdeen. Opened in 2012, the par-72 layout measures more than 7,400 yards from the back tees. Night golf here is a special event rather than a full 18-hole round under floodlights. On March 19 and 20, 2027, the club is scheduled to host two evenings of night golf across five holes of the Old Course, using illuminated golf balls, glowing flags and markers to guide players around the layout after dark. The £75 package includes a burger and beer at The Dunes Restaurant & Bar before tee-off, with tee times advertised between 7pm and 9pm. It offers a different way to experience the world-ranked Aberdeenshire links, with the dunes and coastal setting providing the backdrop to a short round after dark. As the event runs on selected dates, golfers should check the club's website for availability and booking details. Lofoten Links (Nordland, Norway) Located on the island of Gimsøya in Norway's Lofoten archipelago, Lofoten Links is an 18-hole links course set along a rugged stretch of coastline. The sea comes into play on several holes, while sandy beaches and the surrounding mountains add to the setting. At 68 degrees north, Lofoten Links offers a very different kind of night golf. During the summer's midnight-sun period, the sun stays above the horizon, allowing golfers to play at any hour without the need for floodlights. The club's Midnight Sun Unlimited Golf experience allows guests to play for 24 hours at a time, with tee times at 10pm or 2am among the options. In 2023, one guest completed an astonishing 7.5 rounds in 24 hours. It's an unusual opportunity to play a world-ranked links course at a time when most golfers would be heading to bed.
